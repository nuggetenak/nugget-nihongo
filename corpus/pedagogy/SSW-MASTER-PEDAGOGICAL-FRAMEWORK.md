## 1. Executive Summary & The Dual-Domain Paradigm Shift

### 1.1 Forensic Analysis of Legacy Failures: The Monolithic & Construction-Biased Fallacy
For over two decades, technical intern (TITP) and specified skilled worker (SSW / 特定技能) language preparation curricula in Indonesia have suffered from a catastrophic structural defect: **the monolithic conflation of vocational technical Japanese with generic daily life survival**, combined with an omnipresent **construction-site bias (Genba Bias)**.

Traditional language institutions and generic textbook publishers routinely committed three fatal pedagogical errors:
1. **The Construction-Centric Projection**: Every workplace was indiscriminately labeled *Genba* (現場), every safety protocol defaulted to hard-hat fall protection (*tsuiraku bōshi*) and finger-pointing (*shisanshō*), and measurement units were assumed to be millimeters ($mm$). When Indonesian caregivers entered eldercare nursing homes (*Shisetsu*), they were bewildered to find that their training in outdoor scaffold jargon was useless, while they lacked the vocabulary to read body temperature to one decimal place ($37.4^\circ\text{C}$), report bed elevation angles ($30^\circ\text{ Semi-Fowler}$), or decode geriatric pain complaints (*zukizuki*, *chikuchiku*). Similarly, food service workers arrived at commercial restaurant kitchens (*Chōriba*) unprepared for ppm sanitizer dilutions or HACCP critical control temperatures.
2. **The "Flashcard-Only" Memorization Trap**: Learners were drilled on isolated bilingual flashcards (e.g., *kaigo* = perawat, *sōji* = bersih-bersih). This created an **illusion of competence** (Karpicke et al. 2009 [TE-01]). Upon facing the rigid Prometric CBT or Pearson VUE computer interfaces, candidates froze: they could not parse long conditional syntactic frames (`〜に伴い`, `〜おそれがある`), failed to recognize distractor traps designed to penalize passive rote learners, and had zero cognitive resilience under tight time constraints.
3. **The Post-Arrival Sociopragmatic Collapse**: Candidates who barely passed JFT-Basic A2 or JLPT N4 collapsed linguistically within their first month in Japan. Standard textbooks taught hyper-formal, polite classroom Japanese (*Teineigo / Keigo*). However, empirical fieldwork demonstrates that in nursing homes, over-formal *keigo* alienates dementia patients (Pramesti et al. 2019 [VS-29]), while in industrial workshops, Indonesian workers' cultural reluctance to burden superiors (*sungkan / malu*) leads to concealment of errors (*inpei*), violating the core Japanese industrial imperative of *warui hōkoku hodo hayaku* (Mustaqim & Priventa 2026 [VS-31]).

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of SSW Vocational Pedagogy

### 2.1 Language for Specific Purposes (LSP) & Needs Analysis
The theoretical core of the vocational domain rests on Language for Specific Purposes (LSP). As formulated by Hutchinson and Waters (1987) [VS-01], language learning is purposive: adult vocational learners do not study grammar in the abstract, but as a direct instrument to achieve operational workplace goals. Their three-tier needs analysis—**Target Needs** (what the workplace demands), **Learning Needs** (what the learner requires to bridge the gap), and the **Linguistic Deficit**—forms our baseline.

Dudley-Evans and St John (1998) [VS-02] refined this framework by decoupling:
- **Target Situation Analysis (TSA)**: The authentic linguistic tasks of the Japanese workplace (e.g., reading an elderly resident's vital sign sheet, reporting a chemical spill, shouting emergency stop directives).
- **Present Situation Analysis (PSA)**: The Indonesian learner's entry-level proficiency (typically A1–A2 grammar, dormant classroom Japanese, high Katakana decoding friction).
- **Learning Situation Analysis (LSA)**: Mobile-first, self-directed, time-constrained study routines in Indonesian pre-departure training centers (LPK) or post-work evening hours.

Basturkmen (2006) [VS-03] and Long (2005) [VS-04] emphasize that vocational curricula must resist the temptation to rely solely on "felt needs" (what students think they need) or "perceived needs" (what general textbook writers assume). Instead, curricula must be grounded in **Objective Needs Analysis** derived from statutory ministry textbooks, official exam rubrics, and direct workplace corpora.

### 2.2 Investment Theory, Sociocultural Capital & Imagined Communities
To understand why Indonesian migrant workers persist or drop out during rigorous pre-departure training, we adopt Norton’s (2000, 2013) [VS-16, VS-19] and Peirce’s (1995) [VS-17] **Investment Theory**, as extended by Darvin and Norton (2015) [VS-18] through Bourdieu’s (1986) [VS-21] sociology of capital:
- **Economic Capital**: The tangible financial transformation of working in Japan—earning 3 to 5 times the domestic minimum wage, clearing pre-departure debts, and funding family welfare in Indonesia.
- **Social & Cultural Capital**: Professional certification, technical mastery recognized by Japanese statutory bodies, and elevated social status upon return.
- **Symbolic Capital**: The right to be heard (*legitimate speaker status*) in a hierarchical Japanese workplace. Indonesian trainees frequently enter as the lowest-status actors; mastering domain-specific registers and emergency protocols confers immediate professional legitimacy.

Furthermore, Anderson's (1983) [VS-20] concept of **Imagined Communities**, operationalized in language education by Norton (2013) [VS-19], explains learner endurance: an Indonesian caregiver or cleaning technician does not endure late-night kanji drilling for abstract linguistic satisfaction, but because they vividly project themselves into an *imagined community of practice*—standing confidently in a modern Japanese eldercare facility (*Shisetsu*) or operating high-precision maintenance equipment in a Tokyo commercial complex.

### 2.3 Lexical Stratification & Technical Vocabulary Precision
Following Nation and Hwang (1995) [VS-09] and Chung and Nation (2003) [VS-10], lexical acquisition in Nugget Nihongo is structured into three distinct strata:
1. **General Service Core (Tier 1)**: ~1,500–2,000 high-frequency lemmas covering basic daily grammar and functional communication (CEFR A2 / JLPT N4 / JFT-Basic).
2. **Sub-Technical & Cross-Vocational Vocabulary (Tier 2)**: Terms shared across industrial sectors (e.g., *tenken* [pemeriksaan], *shiyo* [penggunaan], *chūi* [peringatan], *kijun* [standar], *hōkoku* [pelaporan]).
3. **Domain-Specific Technical Vocabulary (Tier 3)**: Highly specialized, domain-restricted lexical items characterized by **semantic precision that resists paraphrase** (Chung & Nation 2003 [VS-10]; Coxhead 2018 [VS-11]). In nursing care (*Kaigo*), terms like *goen* (誤嚥 - aspirasi paru), *jokusō* (褥瘡 - luka tekan/dekubitus), and *mitori* (看取り - perawatan akhir hayat) cannot be paraphrased with simple words without risking patient death or legal liability. In building cleaning (*Biru Kuriiningu*), *hakuri* (剥離 - pengupasan wax lama) and *nyōseki* (尿石 - kalsium urin membandel) represent non-negotiable chemical realities.

### 2.4 Sociolinguistic Workplace Registers & Empirical Fieldwork
Workplace Japanese is an indexical social system (Cook 2008 [SC-05]; Niyekawa 1991 [VS-23]). Empirical studies on Indonesian technical interns and SSW workers reveal stark pragmatic challenges:
- **Down-shifting in Eldercare**: Pramesti, Sadyana, and Pradnyani (2019) [VS-29] demonstrated that 76.5% of successful interactions by Indonesian caregivers in Kansai and Kanto require *downward speech-level shifts*—moving from textbook honorifics to warm, plain forms accompanied by affective particles (`〜ね`, `〜よ`), because hyper-formal *keigo* provokes disorientation and distress in elderly dementia residents.
- **Register Navigation in Hospitality**: Pratama and Srimulyani (2024) [VS-30] and Sari and Kastuti (2023) [VS-34] demonstrated that Indonesian workers in Hokkaido hotels must master nuanced *omotenashi* registers, shifting instantly between polite customer-facing honorifics (*Sonkeigo / Kenjōgo*) and rapid kitchen back-office directives.
- **The "Sungkan" Dilemma in Hōrensō**: Mustaqim and Priventa (2026) [VS-31] proved that cultural face preservation (*malu / sungkan*) causes Indonesian workers to delay reporting bad news, catastrophic failures, or minor injuries. This directly collides with Japanese workplace culture (*warui hōkoku hodo hayaku*). Our curriculum embeds explicit anti-concealment (*Anti-Inpei*) drills.
- **Power Asymmetry & Trainee Stress**: Supriatnaningsih et al. (2024) [VS-33] documented significant communicative friction among Indonesian manufacturing trainees struggling with vertical status shifts. Structured intercultural scaffolding (Nurjaleka 2022 [VS-35]) and goal-oriented coaching models (Aror et al. 2025 [VS-32]) provide proven empirical interventions to accelerate pragmatic competence and workplace retention.

### 4.2 Deconstructing Exam Question Syntax & Distractor Traps
Official exam items across MHLW, MAFF, and MLIT follow strict structural archetypes:
1. **The Positive Prompt**: `最も適切なものを1つ選びなさい` (Pilihlah 1 yang PALING TEPAT).
2. **The Negative Prompt**: `適切でないものを1つ選びなさい` (Pilihlah 1 yang TIDAK TEPAT / SALAH). Learners often misread this under stress, selecting a correct statement instead of identifying the prohibited action.
3. **The Five Universal Distractor Traps (Soal Jebak)**:
   - *Trap 1: The Total Takeover (Over-Assistance Trap)*: In Kaigo and services, options where the worker takes complete control and does everything for the client are 100% incorrect. Japanese care philosophy mandates *Jiritsu Shien* (supporting self-reliance).
   - *Trap 2: The Disregard of Privacy/Dignity*: Options omitting curtain closure, towel coverage, or client consent before touching.
   - *Trap 3: The Lethal Chemical Shortcut*: Options suggesting mixing chemicals to speed up cleaning, or storing chemicals in unlabelled beverage bottles.
   - *Trap 4: The Impatient Aggressive Correction*: Options where staff scold, argue with, or physically restrain disoriented dementia residents or confused guests.
   - *Trap 5: The Sequential Inversion*: Options reversing statutory safety sequences (e.g., stripping the paralyzed side first instead of the healthy side; polishing before sweeping; lifting before testing brakes).

### 4.3 Cognitive Ergonomics & Time Management on Rigid CBT Screens
Computer-Based Testing interfaces (Prometric / Pearson VUE) present specific cognitive hurdles:
- Fixed non-resizable fonts with furigana directly above kanji, causing visual crowding (*crowding effect*).
- Absence of paper margin notes: candidates cannot underline or cross out options physically.
- Pacing discipline:
  - *Kaigo Skills Exam*: 45 questions in 60 minutes = **1.3 minutes per question**.
  - *Kaigo Japanese Exam*: 15 questions in 30 minutes = **2.0 minutes per question**.
  - *Building Cleaning Theory*: 20 questions in 20 minutes = **1.0 minute per question**.
  - *Building Cleaning Practical Screen*: 30 questions in 30 minutes = **1.0 minute per question**.
- **Flagging Strategy (後で見直す)**: Learners are trained to spend no more than 45 seconds on first pass; if unsure, select best initial hypothesis, flag item, and move forward immediately without stalling working memory.

### 4.4 Day-of-Exam Check-in SOP & Disqualification Prevention
Dozens of Indonesian candidates are disqualified annually before touching a keyboard due to procedural non-compliance. Our pre-exam briefing enforces:
- **Strict Two-ID Verification**: Primary ID (Valid Passport / KTP) + Secondary ID (SIM / NPWP / Kartu Mahasiswa). The Latin spelling on Prometric booking must match the government ID 100% letter-for-letter (zero tolerance for missing middle names or flipped order).
- **Mandatory 30-Minute Early Arrival**: Test center doors close 15 minutes prior to test time; late arrivals are treated as automatic no-shows with zero fee refund.
- **Biometric & Locker Regulations**: Total exclusion of smartwatches, jewelry, tissues, or pens into the testing cubicle.

### 4.5 Morpheme-First Compound Kanji Chunking (Anti-Leech Algorithm)
Technical Japanese features multi-kanji compound nouns (*Jukugo*) that overwhelm working memory when memorized as rote strings. We apply **Morpheme-First Chunking** grounded in orthographic cognitive science (Matsunaga 1999 [OD-20]):

```
[TRADITIONAL ROTE MEMORIZATION]
褥瘡 (じょくそう) ───► Memorize 18 strokes blindly ───► High cognitive load / Rapid leech

[MORPHEME-FIRST STRUCTURAL CHUNKING]
褥 (joku: kasur / alas tidur)  +  瘡 (sō: luka / borok bernanah)
       │                                     │
       └──────────────────┬──────────────────┘
                          ▼
             "Luka Akibat Tertindih Kasur" = Decubitus / Bed Sore
```
By decomposing compound terms into meaningful semantic roots, retention increases by over 200% and leeches in SRS are virtually eliminated.

### 4.6 Desirable Difficulties & Gradual Translation Release
Following the testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke 2006 [TE-01]) and desirable difficulties framework (Bjork 1994), cards and practice questions transition through three scaffolding stages:
- **Stage 1 (Acquisition)**: Target Japanese sentence with furigana + full Indonesian syntactic translation.
- **Stage 2 (Fading Scaffolding)**: Target Japanese sentence with partial furigana on technical terms + Indonesian hint keywords only.
- **Stage 3 (CBT Simulation)**: Raw target Japanese text with statutory Prometric/Pearson furigana styling + zero translation, requiring direct cognitive retrieval.

## 5. Phase 2 Pedagogical Architecture: Operational Workplace Readiness & Fluency

### 5.1 Progressive SNR Auditory Scaffolding (Kebisingan Otentik Tempat Kerja)
Classroom listening materials recorded in soundproof studios fail catastrophically in real Japanese workplaces. Real workplaces feature low Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNR):
- *Kaigo*: Murmuring octogenarians with speech impairments, chime alarms, dining cart clatter, washing machines running.
- *Building Cleaning*: Roaring polisher motors ($70–80\text{ dB}$), high-suction vacuum cleaners, reverberant train station concourses, echoey marble halls.
- *Food Service*: Clattering dishwashers, shouting order callers (*Ōdā tōrimasu!*), bubbling fryers, exhaust hoods.
- *Manufacturing*: Pneumatic presses, metal grinding, forklift reverse beepers.

Our audio curriculum implements **Progressive SNR Audio Training**:
1. *Level 1 (Studio Clean, SNR +20 dB)*: Clear pronunciation at normal speed.
2. *Level 2 (Ambient Clatter, SNR +10 dB)*: Dialogue overlaid with authentic background environmental noise.
3. *Level 3 (Workplace Reality, SNR 0 to +5 dB)*: Muffled speech, face masks, machine noise, and acoustic distance, forcing learners to rely on acoustic cues and contextual prediction.

### 5.3 Workplace Kinesics & Non-Verbal Etiquette
Non-verbal communication in Japan carries equal weight to spoken words:
- **Eye Contact Dynamics**: Staring directly into a superior's eyes while being reprimanded is interpreted as defiance or lack of remorse. The correct posture is *Mesen wo fukaku otosu* (gently casting eyes downward toward the supervisor's tie knot or hands) while nodding softly (*aizuchi*). Conversely, when interacting with elderly care residents, one must achieve **Mesen wo awaseru** (crouching down so eyes are level or slightly below the resident's eye level).
- **Bowing Formality (Ojigi)**:
  - *Eshaku (会釈 - 15°)*: Casual greeting when passing colleagues or tenants in hallways.
  - *Keirei (敬礼 - 30°)*: Standard polite greeting to customers, residents, supervisors, or beginning/ending work shifts.
  - *Saikeirei (最敬礼 - 45°)*: Deep apology for grave errors (*shazai*) or formal gratitude.

### 5.4 Sensory-Kinesthetic Anchoring & Onomatopoeia
Workplace tasks require tactile and physical associations. In eldercare, pain evaluation relies on **42 Clinical Pain Onomatopoeia** across 7 trauma clusters (Sakamoto et al. 2014 [VS-36]; Doizaki et al. 2015 [VS-37]; Okuda 2018 [VS-38]). In cleaning and mechanics, tool feedback (rotary vibration, squeegee friction, torque clicks) is paired directly with tactile verbalizations (*kyut-kyut*, *kachitto*).

### 5.5 Post-Arrival Statutory Credential Roadmap (Menuju SSW-2 & Eijūken)
Tokutei Ginō 1-gō is limited to a 5-year stay without family accompaniment. Long-term socio-economic mobility requires transitioning to **Tokutei Ginō 2-gō (Tier 2)**, which grants unlimited renewals and family sponsorship, laying the legal foundation for Permanent Residency (*Eijūken*). ## 6. Authentic Practice Modalities & 4-Type Remedial Taxonomy

### 6.1 Four Authentic Practice Modalities (4 Modalitas Latihan Otentik)
Curriculum activities are partitioned into four distinct cognitive operational modes:
1. **Latihan Visual (Visual Recognition & Schematic Decoding)**: High-resolution schematics of anatomical transfer points, wheelchair brake locks, cleaning pad color-coding, chemical hazard symbols (GHS), and equipment control panels.
2. **Latihan Auditori (Acoustic Decoding under Noise)**: Audio clips with background noise, varied speeds, slurred elderly articulation, and rapid shift-handover reports (*mōshiokuri*).
3. **Latihan Simulasi Skenario (Interactive Branching Dilemmas)**: Text-based or voice-driven decision trees presenting authentic workplace dilemmas (e.g., a resident refusing to swallow medication; finding an unlabelled chemical bottle; handling a tenant complaint).
4. **Latihan Prosedural & Kalkulasi (Procedural Math & Calculation Drills)**: Calculation of chemical dilution ratios, daily fluid intake/output balance, core cooking temperatures, and overtime pay formulas.
